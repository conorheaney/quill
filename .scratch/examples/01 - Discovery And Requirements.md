# Discovery And Requirements

Software work usually starts long before anyone writes code. The earliest phase is often a mix of discovery, problem framing, and requirements gathering. Teams use this stage to decide what problem matters, who is affected by it, what success looks like, and what constraints are already in play. Even in lightweight environments, this step has real value because it reduces the chance of building the wrong thing efficiently.

Discovery is less about documenting everything and more about reducing uncertainty. A team may begin with a vague request such as "make file loading easier" or "improve the editing experience." That request sounds actionable, but it still leaves important questions unanswered. Which users struggle today? What are they trying to do? What currently blocks them? Is the issue speed, reliability, usability, missing capability, or something else entirely? Good discovery turns broad requests into sharper statements that can guide later trade-offs.

Requirements gathering builds on that by describing what the software should do and what conditions it needs to respect. Functional requirements describe expected behavior. A text editor might need to open files, preserve formatting, show a live preview, or warn users before discarding unsaved changes. Non-functional requirements describe qualities or constraints around the behavior. Those might include startup speed, accessibility, auditability, offline use, compatibility with a desktop runtime, or predictable error handling. Teams often miss this distinction when moving quickly, but the distinction matters because a feature can appear complete while still failing the real quality bar.

Stakeholder input is important here, but it should not be treated as a flat list of opinions. Product owners, users, developers, testers, and support staff each see different parts of the problem. Product owners may understand business outcomes. Users expose pain points and edge cases. Developers highlight technical constraints and dependency risks. Testers often spot ambiguity early because vague requirements are hard to verify. Useful requirements work brings those perspectives together without letting the document turn into a catch-all parking lot.

A practical way to improve this phase is to ask a few grounding questions. What is changing? Why now? What would make this effort successful? What should explicitly stay out of scope? What assumptions are being made that could later prove false? These questions do not need a heavyweight workshop. Even short written answers can create clarity that protects the rest of the lifecycle from churn.

This phase also benefits from examples. Concrete examples make abstract requirements easier to understand and easier to test later. If a team says "the app should handle complex markdown reliably," that sounds sensible but vague. If the team instead says "the app should open markdown files containing fenced code blocks, tables, links, and images without leaving preview or outline panes in a broken state," the requirement becomes much more actionable. Examples expose hidden complexity and usually reveal what the team actually cares about.

Prioritization belongs here too. Not every requirement deserves equal weight. Some items are essential because they directly support the user goal. Others are risk reducers, quality checks, or nice-to-have improvements. Early prioritization helps teams avoid false equivalence, where small polish work competes unfairly with core product behavior. It also makes later planning easier because the team already understands what must be delivered first if time gets tight.

Requirements are not meant to freeze learning. In iterative teams, they act more like an agreed starting point than a permanent contract. As design, implementation, and testing progress, the team may learn that something needs to be refined. That is normal. The goal of a good requirements phase is not to predict every detail perfectly. The goal is to create enough shared understanding that later decisions are deliberate rather than accidental.

When this phase is done well, downstream work becomes more focused. Design has a clearer target. Implementation has fewer surprises. Testing has something concrete to verify. Release decisions have a stronger basis. When it is skipped or rushed, the team tends to pay for it later through rework, conflicting expectations, and unclear acceptance decisions.

For scratch testing, this kind of document is useful because it mixes headings, paragraphs, emphasis opportunities, and concept-heavy prose without depending on a specific product workflow. It also gives you a realistic medium-length file that still reads naturally when loaded into a markdown editor or preview pane.
